The Social Physics Tradition: From Comte to Pentland to Mul-
tiplicity

The term "social physics" has a rich and contested history. It was first coined by Auguste
Comte (1798–1857), the French philosopher who also gave us the word "sociology." Comte
envisioned a science of society modeled on the natural sciences—one that would discover the
general laws governing social organization and change, and use those laws to direct society
toward a moral, orderly, and just future.

Comte’s system rested on the Law of Three Stages: theological, metaphysical, and pos-
itive.
He divided sociology into social statics (the study of social structures) and social
dynamics (the study of social change). His vision was audacious: sociology would be "like
Newton’s physics, formulating the laws of the social universe."

Two centuries later, Alex Pentland (b. 1952), a professor at MIT, revived the term. For
Pentland, social physics is about idea flow—"the way human social networks spread ideas and
transform those ideas into behaviors." He identifies two key processes: exploring (exposure to
novel ideas) and engagement (face-to-face social interaction where ideas take root). Pentland
argues that the proliferation of digital data now makes a quantitative social physics possible.

9.2.2
Exploring and Engagement

Pentland’s core insight is the interplay of two distinct types of information propagation:

1. Exploring — Exposure to novel ideas. This is the "wide search" phase, where diversity
of input is crucial. Pentland argues that "when you’re exploring new ideas, you want to
take everything you possibly can, because there’s really no cost to that."

2. Engagement — Face-to-face social interaction where nonverbal communication plays a
crucial role. "Adopting habits is a very conservative process that seems to be driven very
largely by social learning, by seeing other people doing the same thing."

The importance of balancing exploration and engagement emerges repeatedly in Pent-
land’s research. Too much exploration without engagement leads to fragmentation; too much
engagement without exploration leads to stagnation.
